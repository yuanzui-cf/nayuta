Most people know me as the overnight sensation who won the GF Newcomer Award at age 18. They see the royalty checks, the anime adaptations, the reprint stickers plastered across bookstore displays.

What they don't know — or perhaps what they prefer to gloss over as mere "artistic angst" — is that just a few short years ago, I had already decided that my life was over.

---

## The Endless Winter of Middle School

I don't talk about my middle school days very often. Even now, the memory has a metallic, bloody taste to it.

I was awkward, quiet, and unable to read social cues. In the cruel, miniature ecosystem of a classroom, weakness is smelled like blood in shark-infested waters. The whispers started first, followed by the stolen shoes, the cruel scrawls across my desk, and eventually the casual physical violence that adults conveniently label as _"juvenile misunderstandings."_

I stopped going to school. I locked my door. I pulled down the heavy blackout curtains and refused to turn on the ceiling lamp.

Months bled into years. I existed in a state of living decay. My parents didn't know what to do; they left trays of food outside my door, which I ate like a ghost in the dark. I didn't want to die, but I desperately, agonizingly did not want to be alive.

## The Paperback with the Ridiculous Premise

One afternoon, my mother slipped a bag of secondhand light novels through the door crack, hoping against hope that something might spark a reaction. Most of them were generic fantasies that slid off my numb brain like rainwater on glass.

And then, I opened a book written by a completely unknown debut author named **Itsuki Hashima**.

On the surface, it was loud, eccentric, and thoroughly shameless. It was obsessed with little sisters, packed with juvenile comedy, and completely unhinged. Critics at the time probably dismissed it as trashy pulp.

```
"Even if the whole world turns its back on you,
 even if nobody believes in the person you want to become —
 as long as you have one undeniable passion,
 you are allowed to stand up and roar."
```

When I hit those lines, my breath caught in my throat.

The prose wasn't polished. It wasn't the refined literature they forced us to dissect in middle school grammar classes. But it was **alive**. It was screaming. It was bleeding raw human heart onto the cheap paper pages. The author wasn't writing to win prestige or impress intellectuals; he was writing because if he didn't pour his madness onto paper, he would choke on it.

For the first time in three years, warm tears rolled down my hollow cheeks.

<Callout type="note" title="A Revelation in the Dark">
  In that dark, suffocating room, Hashima-senpai's words reached through the
  void, grabbed me by the shoulders, and dragged my soul kicking and screaming
  back into the daylight.
</Callout>

## The Birth of Kani Nayuta

I stood up. My knees shook. My eyes stung against the dim glow of an old laptop screen.

I opened a blank text editor, placed my fingers onto the keys, and whispered aloud:

> _"If a story can save someone like me... then I want to build worlds, too. I want to become someone worthy of standing in the same world as Hashima Itsuki."_

That night, the first sentence of _The Silvery Landscape_ was born.

To this day, people ask why I pursue Hashima-senpai with such shameless, uninhibited affection. Why I tease him, why I demand his cuddles, why I declare my love at the top of my lungs in front of editors and strangers alike.

The answer is embarrassingly simple:

He didn't just write a book. He breathed life back into a corpse. My heart beats because of him; therefore, every beat belongs to him. It's not a complicated equation. It's just gravity.
